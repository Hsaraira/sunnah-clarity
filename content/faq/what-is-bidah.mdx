## The Short Answer

Bid'a (بدعة) literally means "innovation" or "something new." In Islamic law, the vast majority of classical scholars — including [Imam al-Nawawi](/scholars/imam-al-nawawi), [al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam](/scholars/al-izz-ibn-abd-al-salam), [Imam al-Shafi'i](/scholars/imam-al-shafii), and [Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani](/scholars/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani) — divide bid'a into categories ranging from obligatory to prohibited, following the same five-fold classification used for all actions in Islamic law.

The common claim that "every single innovation is forbidden" is a misreading of the hadith in Sahih Muslim. The very scholars who compiled, narrated, and commented on this hadith explicitly explain that the general statement is qualified by other evidence — a foundational principle of Islamic jurisprudence.

## The Key Evidence

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Every innovation is misguidance" (Sahih Muslim). However, [Imam al-Nawawi](/scholars/imam-al-nawawi) — the primary commentator on Sahih Muslim itself — explains in his commentary that this is a *general statement qualified by specifics*. He states directly that bid'a is divided into five categories: obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and prohibited.

[Umar ibn al-Khattab (رضي الله عنه)](/scholars/umar-ibn-al-khattab), the second Caliph, called the congregation of tarawih prayer "an excellent bid'a" (Sahih al-Bukhari) — demonstrating that the Companions themselves understood innovation as having different categories.

## Why This Matters

Understanding bid'a properly is essential because the blanket condemnation of all innovation has been used to attack practices that the scholarly majority has accepted for centuries — including celebrating the mawlid, collective dhikr, and following the methodology of the four madhabs.
