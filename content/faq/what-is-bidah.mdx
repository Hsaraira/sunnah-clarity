## The Short Answer

Bid'a (بدعة) literally means "innovation" or "something new." In Islamic law, the vast majority of classical scholars — including [Imam al-Nawawi](/scholars/imam-al-nawawi), [al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam](/scholars/al-izz-ibn-abd-al-salam), [Imam al-Shafi'i](/scholars/imam-al-shafii), and [Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani](/scholars/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani) — divide bid'a into categories ranging from obligatory to prohibited, following the same five-fold classification used for all actions in Islamic law.

The claim that "every single innovation is forbidden" is a misreading of one hadith in Sahih Muslim, contradicted by how the hadith's own commentators explain it.

## The Hadith Everyone Cites

<HadithBlock
  arabic="كُلُّ بِدْعَةٍ ضَلَالَةٌ"
  translation="Every innovation is misguidance."
  source="Sahih Muslim, no. 867 (from the Jumu'a khutba of the Prophet ﷺ)"
  narrator="Jabir ibn Abdullah (رضي الله عنه)"
/>

This hadith is authentic and undisputed. The question is how to understand "every" (*kull*). In Arabic juristic methodology, general statements (*'amm*) are routinely qualified by specific evidence (*khass*). The scholars who compiled and transmitted this hadith explain exactly how it is qualified.

## How the Great Commentators Explain It

<ScholarQuote
  scholar="Imam al-Nawawi"
  scholarSlug="imam-al-nawawi"
  title="Greatest commentator on Sahih Muslim"
  dates="d. 676 AH / 1278 CE"
  source="Sharh Sahih Muslim, commentary on this very hadith"
  quote="This is a general statement that is particularized (hadha 'amm makhsus). What is meant is the majority of innovations. The linguists say: bid'a is anything done without a prior model. Scholars say it is five types: obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and prohibited."
/>

This is not an obscure opinion. This is the primary commentary on Sahih Muslim — the book that contains the hadith itself. Al-Nawawi then gives examples of each category:

- **Obligatory bid'a**: codifying Arabic grammar to protect the Quran from misrecitation; compiling the Quran into a single bound volume (mushaf)
- **Recommended bid'a**: building madrasas (Islamic schools), writing books of Islamic knowledge, organizing tarawih prayer in congregation
- **Prohibited bid'a**: sects that contradict the established creed of Ahl al-Sunna

<ScholarQuote
  scholar="Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani"
  scholarSlug="ibn-hajar-al-asqalani"
  title="Greatest commentator on Sahih al-Bukhari"
  dates="d. 852 AH / 1449 CE"
  source="Fath al-Bari, commentary on Sahih al-Bukhari"
  quote="The root meaning of 'innovation' is what is produced without a prior model. In Sacred Law it is applied to what has no origin in a legal proof. For scholars of the Sunna, innovations are further divided: if it falls under something good in the Shari'a, it is a good innovation; if it falls under something evil, it is an evil innovation; otherwise it is permissible."
/>

<ScholarQuote
  scholar="Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam"
  scholarSlug="al-izz-ibn-abd-al-salam"
  title="Sultan of the Scholars"
  dates="d. 660 AH / 1262 CE"
  source="Qawa'id al-Ahkam fi Masalih al-Anam"
  quote="Bid'a is divided into obligatory, prohibited, recommended, disliked, and permissible. The way to determine this is to weigh the innovation against the principles of the Sacred Law: if it falls under the principles of obligation, it is obligatory; if under the principles of prohibition, it is prohibited; and so on."
/>

## The Proof from the Companions

The classification of bid'a isn't just theoretical. The Companions themselves used the word "bid'a" in a positive sense:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="نِعْمَتِ الْبِدْعَةُ هَذِهِ"
  translation="What an excellent bid'a this is!"
  source="Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 2010"
  narrator="Umar ibn al-Khattab (رضي الله عنه), referring to the congregational tarawih prayer"
/>

Umar — the second Caliph and one of the greatest Companions — gathered the Muslims behind one imam for tarawih and called it a bid'a *approvingly*. If "every bid'a" were categorically forbidden, Umar would be contradicting the Prophet ﷺ — which is inconceivable.

## The Prophetic Encouragement of Good Innovation

The Prophet ﷺ himself distinguished between good and bad innovations:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="مَنْ سَنَّ فِي الْإِسْلَامِ سُنَّةً حَسَنَةً فَلَهُ أَجْرُهَا وَأَجْرُ مَنْ عَمِلَ بِهَا بَعْدَهُ"
  translation="Whoever introduces a good practice (sunna hasana) in Islam, he will have its reward and the reward of those who act upon it after him, without their rewards being diminished in the least."
  source="Sahih Muslim, no. 1017"
  narrator="Jarir ibn Abdullah (رضي الله عنه)"
/>

This hadith explicitly encourages Muslims to introduce good practices. The word *sanna* (سنّ) means "to establish" or "to introduce" — it refers to starting something that did not exist before, provided it aligns with the principles of the religion.

## Why the "All Innovation Is Haram" Claim Is Problematic

If every new thing in religion were categorically forbidden, the following would all be haram:

1. **Diacritical marks on the Quran** — added by Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali (d. 69 AH) to prevent misrecitation; no one objects to this
2. **Hadith collections in book form** — Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, and every other hadith book were compiled long after the Prophet ﷺ died
3. **Usul al-fiqh** (principles of jurisprudence) — systematized by [Imam al-Shafi'i](/scholars/imam-al-shafii) centuries later
4. **Building minarets on mosques** — not done in the Prophet's time
5. **Two adhans for Jumu'a** — introduced by Uthman (رضي الله عنه), the third Caliph

Every Muslim alive today benefits from innovations that serve the religion. The proper question is never "Is this new?" but "Does this new practice align with or contradict the principles of Sacred Law?"

    </>
  }
/>

  claim="If the Prophet ﷺ didn't do it, it can't be part of Islam."
